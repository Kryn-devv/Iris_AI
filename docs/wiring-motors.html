Six Wires, Two Drivers

Every connection from the ESP32 to two BTS7960 modules on a four-wheel skid-steer robot — and, just as usefully, the four mistakes you cannot make because the calibration page undoes them for you.

Board ESP32 DevKit Drivers 2 × BTS7960 Motors 4, two per side Firmware esp32-iris-node-bts7960

Six signal wires, one 5 V, one ground. Everything else is the battery.

Three wires per driver — RPWM, LPWM and one enable — plus 5 V and a shared ground. The 12 V battery never touches the ESP32. And you do not have to get the sides or the directions right: swap, invert A, invert B and the two trim sliders on the board's own page fix all of that afterwards, without moving a wire.

The DevKit has a pin labelled EN. It is not that EN.

The ESP32 board's own EN pin is the chip's reset. The modules' enable inputs go to GPIO 27 and GPIO 14. This is worth saying out loud precisely because "connect EN to EN" is such a reasonable thing to read off a diagram.

If a module ever responds sometimes

The ESP32's logic is 3.3 V and the module's is nominally 5 V, and most of these boards accept that happily — yours already did, when the plain test sketch drove these motors. But if one module is ever intermittent when the signal is provably good, read the marking on the wide 20-pin chip next to the eight-pin header. A 74HC244 or 74AC244 running on a 5 V rail wants 3.5 V to call an input high, which your 3.3 V is not; moving that module's VCC to the ESP32's 3.3 V pin puts the whole chain back in spec. A 74HCT244 is happy as it is and wants to stay on 5 V. This is the one real explanation for "it worked yesterday".

03The signal side: ESP32 to both modules

Six blue wires and one amber one. Nothing crosses, nothing branches except the 5 V, and every wire is a straight run — this is genuinely the whole logic side of the robot.

Don't save two wires by tying the enables to 3.3 V

It works, and it is what a lot of wiring guides suggest, and it costs you more than it looks. The BTS7960's over-temperature and short-circuit shutdown latches, and the only thing that clears the latch is taking the enable low again. Tie it permanently high and your first thermal trip is permanent until you unplug the battery. You also lose coasting entirely — every stop and every failsafe becomes locked wheels — and during a current-limit event the PWM inputs are ignored, so a stalled motor cannot be stopped at all. Two wires. Worth it.

04Power and ground

This is the half that can destroy something, and it is also the half with only one real rule: every ground in the robot is the same ground. The battery minus, both modules' GND, both modules' B−, and the ESP32's GND all meet.

Where the ESP32 itself gets power

While you are calibrating on the bench, USB is the easy answer — the ESP32 runs from your laptop, the motors run from the battery, and the two only meet at ground. For the robot to drive around untethered you want a 5 V buck converter off the same 12 V battery into the ESP32's 5 V / VIN pin. Either way, the same 5 V rail is what feeds the two modules' VCC.

Four things that end with buying a new part

  • Anything from the 12 V side touching the ESP32. Not B+, not B−, not M+, not M−. The only wires that cross between the two halves of this robot are the six logic signals, one 5 V and one ground.
  • 12 V into 5V / VIN — even though the internet will tell you VIN takes 7–12 V. The regulator survives it and then cooks, and the symptom is a board that resets whenever WiFi transmits. Which is exactly the fault that gets blamed on the firmware for a week.
  • Battery reversed into B+ / B−. There is no protection on the module. It destroys the driver and shorts the battery in the same instant. Meter the polarity at the screw terminals before the connector goes on — every time.
  • Motor current through thin wire. The 43 A printed on the module is the chip's internal current-limit threshold, not what that little PCB will carry — plan on 10–15 A. Size the battery-to-B+ and M-to-motor runs for both of that side's motors stalled, not running: 14–16 AWG for a typical 12 V geared chassis. Signal wire belongs only on the eight-pin header.

Worth adding while you have it apart

None of these three is needed to make the robot drive. All three are the difference between a robot that drives and a robot you can leave running.

05The order to do it in

  1. Battery disconnected first

    Not switched off — disconnected. Every step below is done with no 12 V anywhere in the robot.

  2. Grounds, before anything else

    All to one point at the battery minus. Heavy wire from there to each module's B−; a separate thin wire from there (or from the ESP32) to each module's header GND. Doing grounds first means every later measurement has something honest to measure against.

  3. 5 V to both modules' VCC

    From the ESP32's 5 V pin, or from your buck converter. This is the pin people forget, and forgetting it looks exactly like a broken module.

  4. The six signal wires

    Module A: 25 → RPWM, 26 → LPWM, 27 → the joined R_EN + L_EN. Module B: 32, 33, 14 in the same order.

  5. Motors to M+ / M−

    Each side's two motors in parallel onto that side's module. Don't think about which way round — that is what invert is for.

  6. Three measurements, before any battery meter

    With the battery still disconnected: continuity from each module's header GND to its B− screw (on most boards these are one net — knowing which yours is tells you what your ground wiring actually did); resistance from each logic input to GND (around 10 kΩ if you fitted the pull-downs, near-open if you did not); and polarity at the battery connector, red probe on the wire that is about to meet B+.

  7. Battery on, last wheels up

    Wheels off the ground — and keep them off for the whole of the next step. A boot twitch, a missing VCC and a floating input can all move a motor with no command sent at all.

  8. Power up and open the board's page

    Read the address from Serial Monitor at 115200, or join the robot's own network iris-robot / iriscalib and open http://192.168.4.1. Then press A fwd and B fwd — that one test tells you more than any amount of staring at the wiring.

06What you do not have to get right

This is worth reading before you start, because it changes how carefully you need to work. Four things that look like wiring decisions are not:

Press SAVE and it survives reboots. What the page cannot fix is a missing VCC, a ground that is not common, a motor lead that has fallen off, or a battery that is not connected — which is exactly why those are the first things this sheet asks you to check.

Pins the board will refuse

If you re-assign a pin, the firmware only accepts GPIOs that can actually drive an output. It refuses 6–11 (wired to the flash chip), 20, 24, 28–31 (not present on the package), 34–39 (input only) and 1/3 (the serial port). It accepts but warns about 0, 2, 12 and 15, because the bootloader reads those while the board starts and a driver holding one the wrong way can stop it booting at all.
