Twelve Volts, Three Loads

Wiring one 12 V battery through a 4-channel relay module to a 12 V light, a 3 V fan and a servo — and how the ESP32 switches all three.

Source one 12 V battery Loads 12 V · 3 V · servo Mains none Board iris-node

There is no AC in this build, so there is nothing to convert.

You asked how to get from AC to DC. You don't have to: your only power source is a 12 V battery, and every load is 12 V or lower. No plug, no mains, no live wiring. A relay can switch AC — which is why every tutorial opens with a warning — but switching a 12 V strip and a 3 V fan uses the same relay with none of the risk. What you actually need is the opposite job: turning one 12 V battery into the three different DC voltages your three loads want.

01One battery, three rails

A “rail” is just a wire everything of that voltage shares. Two cheap buck converter modules make the two lower rails, and each converter is a small board with a screw on it you turn to set the output voltage.

02What a relay channel actually is

This is the part that reads as confusing and isn't. A relay is a switch that a wire can press. Each channel has three screw terminals — COM, NO, NC — and the whole of its behaviour is which two of them are joined.

03All four channels at once

Now it is just figure 2, four times, with a different rail arriving at each COM. Each channel is completely independent of the others — nothing is shared except the ground.

04Build it in this order

The order matters more than the wiring does, because two of these steps are where parts get destroyed by being done late.

  1. Wire the 12 V side, battery still disconnected

    Battery lead → fuse holder → switch → and on to the inputs of both buck converters. Leave both converter outputs bare — nothing connected to them yet.

  2. Set buck #1 to 5.0 V, with nothing attached to itdo not skip

    Battery on, switch on. Multimeter on buck #1's output. Turn the little brass screw until it reads 5.0 V. Switch off. A converter out of the box can be set anywhere from 1.25 V to nearly its input voltage — you have no idea what yours is set to until you measure it.

  3. Set buck #2 to 3.2 V the same waythis is the one

    Same procedure, nothing attached. Connecting the 3 V fan before you set this screw is how a 3 V fan dies in one second — if the converter happens to be set near 12 V, the fan gets four times its rated voltage the instant you switch on. Switch off and disconnect the battery when both read right.

  4. Now wire the loads

    Rails into the four COM terminals, appliance + wires out of the NO terminals, NC left empty — figure 3. Put the 1N4007 across the fan while you are there (figure 4).

  5. Wire the ESP32 and the star ground

    The seven thin wires from the table above, then every − in the build to one point: battery −, both converters' − out, ESP32 GND, module GND, and all three appliance − wires.

  6. Battery on

    The relay module's power LED lights, the ESP32 prints its IP on the serial monitor, and all four channels start off — the firmware forces them off before it does anything else, so nothing twitches at power-up.

A 2 A module survives the ordinary case and browns out the ESP32 halfway through a servo move. That reads as “the board keeps rebooting when the curtain moves”, which sends you looking at your code for a week. A 3 A module has the headroom. The 12 V light and the 3 V fan each sit on their own rail and are small, so a single 5 A fuse covers the whole system comfortably while still being far below what the wire can carry.

06Three things to know before you switch it on

The fan needs that diode

A DC motor's magnetic field collapses when the relay opens and drives a reverse voltage spike back down the wire. It arcs the relay contacts and can reset the ESP32. Put the 1N4007 across the fan's own two terminals, stripe on the + side. It sits idle during normal running and absorbs the spike on switch-off.

Nothing motorised comes off an ESP32 pin

Not the fan, not the servo. The 3.3 V regulator on the board cannot source a motor's current, and the 5V pin is just the incoming supply passed straight through — hanging a servo on it drags the board's own supply down with it and the board resets. Both get their power from the 5 V rail, through the relay.

A 3.3 V pin driving a 5 V relay usually works, and sometimes doesn't

The relay module's opto-input is designed around 5 V logic, and the ESP32's pins are 3.3 V. Most modules trigger fine anyway. If one channel never clicks while the others do, that is the cause — not your wiring. The fix is a module labelled “3V3”, or a 4-channel level shifter. Don't go back over your soldering looking for it.

07Once it's wired

Flash firmware/esp32-iris-node/, note the IP it prints, and tell IRIS about it once:

add device home at 192.168.1.73 as relay

turn on the light          light chalu karo
switch off the fan         fan band karo
open the curtain           curtain kholo
close the curtain          parda kholo
open the curtain halfway   set the servo to 45 degrees

The servo's power channel closes for the move and opens again when it finishes, so an idle servo is genuinely off instead of buzzing against its own gearbox. Ask it to hold only when you want it to keep pushing.
